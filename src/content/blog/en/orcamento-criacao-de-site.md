You send a handful of vendors the same message ("how much for a website?") and get back answers that have nothing in common. One comes with a number and almost no description. Another asks for a meeting. Another sends a polished PDF that says nothing about the domain, hosting or who writes the copy.

Most of the time, the problem isn't the vendors. It's the request. When a website design quote is requested without context, each company pictures a different project and prices what it pictured. The result is proposals you can't compare.

This guide shows how to request a website quote the right way: what to include, a ready-to-copy brief template, what every proposal needs to spell out, how to compare different prices without falling into a trap, and the red flags worth a second conversation. If your question is what drives the price up or down, the article on [how much a business website costs](/blog/quanto-custa-site-institucional) complements this one.

## Why the request shapes the quality of the proposal

Without information, a vendor has two options: guess or ask. Those who guess tend to quote the bare minimum to look competitive, and whatever was left out comes back later as an extra charge. Those who ask take longer to reply, but the proposal you get describes your project.

A well-written request makes every vendor quote the same thing and shows right away who actually read what you wrote.

## What to include when you request a website quote

You don't need to know anything about technology to ask well. You need to know what your business wants from the website. These are the points that change the quote:

**Goal of the website.** What the visitor should do: request a quote, call or text, book an appointment, learn about the company before a meeting, find a sales rep. A website exists for one main action. If you know which one, the vendor knows how to design the pages.

**Audience.** Businesses or consumers, one city or the whole country. This changes the tone, the structure and the searches the site needs to rank for.

**Pages.** A first list, even a rough one: home, about, one page per main service, contact, blog, location pages. It doesn't have to be final. The vendor can suggest changes, but needs a starting point.

**Content.** Who writes the copy and where the photos come from. This is the item that changes scope the most and delays projects the most. Be clear about whether the copy already exists, whether you'll write it or whether you expect the vendor to produce it.

**Integrations.** What the site needs to connect to: CRM, the sales team's phone or chat, a booking tool, ERP, product catalog, email marketing platform. If you don't know the technical name, describe what should happen ("when someone fills out the form, a salesperson gets an alert").

**Timeline.** When you need the site live and why: a launch, a trade show, a campaign. A deadline with a reason helps prioritize what matters.

**Current website.** If you have one, send the address and say what works and what doesn't. It also tells the vendor there will be a migration and that old URLs need redirects so you don't lose your Google rankings.

**References.** A few websites you like, from any industry, and why. "It's easy to find the phone number" helps more than "I want something modern."

**Who decides.** Who approves the layout and the copy. More approvers mean more review rounds, and that affects the timeline.

## Website brief template, ready to copy

Copy the template below, fill in what you know and send the same text to every vendor. Where you don't know, write "not sure" and ask for a recommendation.

```text
WEBSITE BRIEF

Company: [name, city, what you sell]
Current website: [URL or "none"]

Goal: [main visitor action: request a quote / call / book]
Problem to solve: [e.g.: customers can't find us on Google]
Audience: [businesses / consumers] Area: [city, state, country]

Pages: [home, about, one per service (list them), contact, blog, locations]

Copy: [we have it / we'll write it / you write it]
Photos: [our own / we need a photo shoot / stock images]
Brand identity: [logo and brand guide / logo only / none]

Integrations: [form to email, CRM (which one) or chat; booking; ERP]
Timeline: [target date and reason]
References: [websites we like and why]
After launch: [who updates the site; do we need maintenance?]
Who approves layout and copy: [names and roles]

Please make sure the proposal details: pages included, who produces the content,
technical SEO items, hosting, domain registration, code handover,
maintenance, support, warranty, timeline per phase and recurring costs.
```

The last paragraph is the most important one: it states what you expect to see in the proposal. A vendor who ignores it has already shown you how they work.

## What every website proposal needs to spell out

A good proposal is a document you can hold the vendor to later. Check that every item below is in writing, not just mentioned on a call:

| Item | What the proposal should say |
|---|---|
| Scope | What's included and, with the same care, what's not |
| Pages | The list, page by page, with what each one will have |
| Content | Who writes the copy, who provides the photos and how many review rounds are included |
| Technical SEO | The concrete items: titles and meta descriptions, clean URLs, sitemap, structured data, Search Console setup, redirects from the old site |
| Mobile and speed | How the site will be tested on phones and how speed will be measured before handover |
| Hosting | Where the site will run, whose name the account is in and who pays |
| Domain | That the domain is registered in your company's name, not the vendor's |
| Code handover | That the code and every login are handed over to you at the end |
| Maintenance | What's included after launch, whether it's optional and how it's billed |
| Support | Which channel you use to ask for help and how quickly you get an answer |
| Warranty | The period in which defects are fixed at no cost and what counts as a defect |
| Timeline | Delivery date and the deadline for each phase, not just the final date |
| Payment | Installments tied to deliverables, not to the calendar |

Two items deserve special attention because they're the ones that most often lock businesses into a vendor. The first is the domain: it's your company's address on the internet and must be registered in your company's name, with a registrar login you control. The second is code and access: hosting, domain, Google Analytics and Search Console should be in your company's accounts, or at least with you as an administrator. If you ever want to switch vendors, that's what lets you do it without starting over.

Don't confuse warranty with maintenance. A warranty covers what was delivered and doesn't work as agreed. Maintenance covers updates, tweaks and new things.

## How to compare website proposals with different prices

With the proposals in hand, the temptation is to look at the total and pick the one in the middle. That usually goes wrong, because proposals with different prices tend to describe different work. Compare scope first and price second.

**Build a comparison spreadsheet.** One row per item in the table above and one column per vendor. Mark "not stated" when an item is missing. It quickly becomes clear who was specific and who left things open.

**Level the playing field.** If one proposal includes copywriting and another doesn't, the price gap has an explanation. Ask the vendor who left it out what it would cost to add, or ask the other for a version without it. Only compare prices once the scopes match.

**Add up recurring costs.** Hosting, domain, business email, theme and plugin licenses, monthly maintenance. A proposal with a lower upfront price and a high monthly fee can cost more over time. Ask for the list of recurring costs, item by item.

**Ask what happens if you leave.** If you cancel maintenance or switch vendors, does the site stay online? Do you keep the code? Proprietary platforms can make sense in some cases, but you need to know before you sign.

**Look at how each vendor got to the price.** Whoever asked questions, wanted to see your current site and suggested cutting or adding something understood the project. Whoever replied with a number and no questions is quoting a generic website.

**Check their published work.** Open the portfolio sites on your phone. See if they load fast and if the forms work. For objective speed criteria, the guide on [slow websites and Core Web Vitals](/blog/site-lento-core-web-vitals) explains how to measure.

**Negotiate scope, not just price.** If the best proposal is above what you can invest right now, ask to split it into phases: a first version with the pages that bring in customers, and the rest afterward. That's healthier than asking for a discount on the same scope, which usually gets made up for with less care in execution.

## Red flags in a website proposal

None of these signs condemns a vendor on its own, but together they point to trouble:

- **A price before any questions.** If the reply to your brief is just a number, nobody thought about your project.
- **A proposal that ignores the brief.** You asked for a CRM integration and the proposal doesn't mention it. Either it was left out, or it'll show up later as an extra.
- **"Unlimited revisions."** Sounds generous, but it usually means nobody defined when the work ends. Prefer a clear number of rounds and a rule for anything beyond that.
- **SEO without a list of items.** "Google-optimized website" without saying what will be done isn't scope. Also be wary of anyone who guarantees a ranking: nobody controls Google's results.
- **"Free forever" hosting on the vendor's account.** The site is tied to them, and "free" tends to end when you want to leave.
- **Full payment upfront.** The norm is installments tied to deliverables.
- **No exit clause in the contract.** If it doesn't say how you get the code, domain and logins when the contract ends, assume it'll be hard.
- **Pressure to sign.** A discount that expires tomorrow exists so you won't compare.

## After the proposals arrive: how to run the conversation

Pick the most complete proposals and schedule a short call with each vendor. Bring the spreadsheet and ask about the items marked "not stated." Other questions that help:

- What would you change in our brief? What would you cut from the first version?
- Who will work on the project and who is our point of contact?
- How does approval work: do we see the layout before development? Is there a staging environment before going live?
- What happens if the timeline slips because of you? And if it slips because of us, for example, on the copy?

At the end, ask for the chosen proposal to be updated with everything you agreed on. If it isn't in writing, it doesn't exist when it's time to hold someone to it. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many website quotes should I request?

Enough to compare without getting lost. In practice, a few detailed proposals are worth more than many vague ones. What matters is sending the same brief to everyone so the comparison is fair.

### Do I need to know exactly what I want before requesting a quote?

You need to know the business goal, not the technical solution. Say what the site should solve, for whom and by when. The brief template in this article works as a script.

### Why do some vendors ask for a meeting before sending a quote?

Because they want to understand the project before naming a price. With a complete brief in hand, the meeting stays short and is just for clearing up specific questions.

### What should I do when proposals have very different prices?

Compare scope before price. The gap is almost always explained by items one proposal includes and the other doesn't: copywriting, service pages, integrations, technical SEO, maintenance or code handover. Level the scopes and compare again.

### Can I use one vendor's proposal to negotiate with another?

You can use your comparison spreadsheet to show what you expect to see in a proposal. Forwarding another vendor's document isn't good practice. What works is asking everyone for the same level of detail.
