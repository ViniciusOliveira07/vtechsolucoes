There comes a point when the company's system starts getting in the way. The ERP handles the basics, but the process that sets your business apart lives in spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups and side controls. The question comes up in every meeting: is it worth building your own system, or is it better to switch to another off-the-shelf ERP?

Both answers can be right, and both can be expensive if chosen for the wrong reason. This guide shows when the off-the-shelf ERP wins, when custom software wins, why the hybrid path is usually the most sensible, which costs don't show up in the first proposal and how to reduce the risks of building.

## When an off-the-shelf ERP is the best choice

An off-the-shelf ERP packs years of work into processes that are the same in almost every company: invoicing, accounts payable and receivable, bank reconciliation, inventory, purchasing, tax compliance. Rebuilding all that from scratch rarely makes sense.

The off-the-shelf ERP usually wins when:

- **Your process looks like the market's.** If the way you sell, invoice and manage stock is the same as your competitors', the ERP was already built for you.
- **Tax and compliance weigh heavily.** Changes in legislation are the vendor's responsibility, and the vendor updates the system for every customer.
- **You need to start fast.** Rolling out an ERP takes weeks or a few months. Building a complete system takes much longer.
- **There is no team to look after software.** With an ERP, support, servers, backups and updates stay with the vendor.

## When custom software pays off

Custom software wins when the process is exactly what sets the company apart, and forcing it into a generic ERP means making the business worse or living on workarounds. Common signs:

- **Your team keeps the "real system" in spreadsheets** next to the ERP, because it lacks the fields, steps or rules you need.
- **The process is specific to your industry or your company:** field service orders, commission calculations with their own rules, technical proposals, project tracking, a portal for customers or partners.
- **ERP customizations have become a project of their own**, expensive, slow and fragile with every vendor update.
- **License costs grow with your headcount** for people who use only a small part of the system.
- **You want the process to be a company asset**, with rules, data and code under your control.

## The hybrid path: off-the-shelf ERP, custom module and integration

For most small and mid-sized companies, the answer isn't "one or the other." It's keeping the ERP where it is good and building custom only what it doesn't cover, connecting the two through integration.

An example: a maintenance company uses its ERP for billing, invoices and finance. Service orders, the technicians' schedule, photos and the customer's signature live in a web system of its own, used on a phone. When a job is done, the integration sends the ERP what needs to be billed. The ERP stays the source of truth for tax and accounting, and the custom module handles what sets the operation apart.

The advantages of this design:

- **Smaller scope and faster delivery**, because you don't rebuild what already works.
- **Less tax risk**, because invoices and compliance stay in the ERP.
- **Freedom to switch ERPs later**, since the custom module talks to it through integration, not from the inside.
- **Investment in stages**, starting with the process that hurts the most.

## Decision table

| Criterion | Off-the-shelf ERP | Custom software | Hybrid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Process that matches the market | Handles it well | Rebuilds what already exists | The ERP handles this part |
| Process that sets the business apart | Adapts you to the system | Adapts the system to you | The custom module handles this part |
| Time until you start using it | Shorter | Longer | Short for the ERP, in stages for the module |
| Tax and compliance | The vendor's responsibility | Has to be built and maintained | Stays in the ERP |
| Cost over time | Per-user licenses and customizations | Development and maintenance | ERP licenses plus the module |
| Ownership of data and code | The vendor's, with limited export | Yours, if it's in the contract | Tax data in the ERP, your own process is yours |
| Dependency | On the ERP vendor | On the developer, if there is no documentation | Split and easier to replace |

## Total cost of ownership: what the first proposal leaves out

Comparing only the license price with the development price leads to mistakes. Look at the total cost over a few years:

- **Per-user licenses.** ERP pricing usually grows with the number of users and modules. Count how many people will use the system today and three years from now.
- **Customization.** Fields, reports and specific screens in the ERP are billed separately and, in some cases, have to be redone with each new version.
- **Rollout and training.** They apply to both paths and are usually underestimated.
- **Maintenance.** For custom software, this includes fixes, security updates, hosting and small improvements. Ask for this estimate in writing.
- **Integrations.** Almost every scenario will require connecting the system to an online store, bank, CRM or WhatsApp. Include that in the math from the start.
- **Lock-in.** What would it cost to leave? Ask how data is exported, in what format and whether the full history comes with it.
- **Data ownership.** Your customer, sales and operations data must be accessible to you, without depending on a vendor's goodwill.

A practical way to compare proposals: build a spreadsheet with these rows and fill them in for three years, for each path. The difference usually shows up in items nobody mentioned in the sales meeting.

## The risks of custom software and how to reduce them

Building has real risks. They are well known, and almost all of them can be controlled through the contract and the way the work is done.

| Risk | How it shows up | How to reduce it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope that keeps growing | Every meeting adds "just one more screen" and the project never ends | A fixed scope in writing, stating what is in and what is out, with changes handled as a new phase |
| Everything delivered at once | Months without seeing anything work and surprises at the end | Phased delivery, each phase in real use before the next one |
| Depending on a single vendor | Only one company or one person knows how to change the system | Code handed over to you, in a repository you own, built on mainstream technology |
| No documentation | Nobody understands the rules when the vendor changes | Documentation of business rules, setup and integrations as a contract deliverable |
| No internal owner | Nobody in the company sets priorities | One person responsible for the process, with time to validate each delivery |

## Questions to decide

Answer these with your team before asking for proposals:

1. Which processes does your current ERP handle well, and which live in spreadsheets or side controls?
2. Are those processes the same as your competitors', or are they what sets the company apart?
3. How many people use the system today, how many will in three years and how many use only a small part of it?
4. How much do you spend today on ERP customizations and on manual rework?
5. Which tax and compliance obligations must remain a vendor's responsibility?
6. Which systems does all of this need to talk to: online store, CRM, bank, WhatsApp?
7. If the vendor disappeared tomorrow, could you access your data and keep operating?
8. Who in the company will own the process and validate each delivery?

If most answers point to standard processes, stay with the off-the-shelf ERP. If they point to one or two processes that set the business apart, the hybrid path is probably yours. If what sets you apart is in almost everything, then it's worth evaluating a broader system of your own, built in phases.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it worth building your own system for a small business?

It is when there is a specific process the ERP doesn't cover and that costs time or money every month. For standard processes, such as tax and finance, an off-the-shelf ERP is almost always the better option. That is why the hybrid path is the most common in small and mid-sized companies.

### How long does it take to build custom software?

It depends on the scope. A module focused on one process can be in use within weeks, and a broad system takes months. That is why the recommendation is to split it into phases, starting with the process that hurts the most, so every stage already delivers results.

### Does my company keep the code of the custom software?

Only if the contract says so. Require delivery of the source code, in a repository under your control, plus the documentation another team would need to carry on the work.

### Can I switch ERPs after building a custom module?

Yes, and that is one of the benefits of the hybrid path. If the module talks to the ERP through integration, switching ERPs means redoing the integration, not the whole module.

### Is it better to customize the ERP or build a separate module?

For small tweaks, like a field or a report, customizing is usually enough. When customization turns into a large project, expensive and fragile with every update, a custom module integrated with the ERP tends to be more stable and cheaper to maintain.
