Hiring a software development company looks simple until the proposals arrive. One is two pages long, another is thirty. One promises delivery in a month, another asks for six. The prices look nothing alike, and every vendor says "anything is possible".

The problem is that almost every software project that goes wrong was already showing signs at the hiring stage. Vague scope, a contract with no way out, code that never reaches your hands.

This guide shows how to choose a software vendor with clear criteria: the warning signs, what a good proposal must include, how to compare fixed scope and hourly billing, the clauses that protect your company and the questions to ask in the very first meeting.

## Warning signs when hiring a software development firm

Before comparing price and timeline, rule out the proposals that already show a problem:

- **There is no written scope.** If the proposal says "system development as discussed in the meeting", nobody knows what will be delivered. When it is time to bill, each side will remember a different conversation.
- **A price without understanding the problem.** A vendor who sends a fixed price after a 15-minute call is guessing. Either the guess will cost you too much, or it will turn into change orders halfway through.
- **Nothing about code ownership.** If the proposal does not say the source code is yours, assume it is not. Some companies deliver only the running system, hosted with them, and charge you to leave.
- **Everything is "possible" and "simple".** A good vendor says no, says "this is more expensive than it looks" and suggests cutting features. Someone who agrees with everything has not thought it through yet.
- **Not a word about testing or a staging environment.** Without a test environment (staging), you will first see the system in production, with customers using it. Without tests, every fix can break something else.
- **A timeline that does not change with the scope.** If you ask for more features and the timeline stays the same, the timeline was never calculated.
- **An invisible team.** You do not know who will write the code, who manages the project or who to call when something is late.

None of these points alone rules out a vendor. Two or three together do.

## What a good software development proposal includes

A serious proposal is a document you can hold the vendor to. It usually has:

1. **Understanding of the problem.** A summary, in the vendor's own words, of what your company needs and why. If they did not understand the problem, the rest does not matter.
2. **Detailed scope.** Features listed one by one, with who uses each screen and what it does. Ideally with a prototype or sketches of the main screens.
3. **What is out of scope.** As important as what is in. "Does not include a mobile app", "does not include migrating old data".
4. **Phases and deliverables.** The project split into phases, with what you receive and approve in each one.
5. **Timeline per phase**, not just a final date.
6. **Technology and hosting.** Which language the system will be built in, where it will run and who pays for the infrastructure.
7. **Integrations.** Which systems the new software talks to (ERP, CRM, payment gateway) and who provides access.
8. **Acceptance criteria.** How it is decided that a phase is done.
9. **Warranty and support.** What happens after delivery.
10. **Investment and payment terms**, tied to the deliverables.

If the proposal does not have at least items 2, 3, 4 and 8, ask for them before comparing prices. Comparing the price of proposals with different scopes is comparing different things.

## Fixed scope or hourly billing: which model to choose

Both models work. What goes wrong is using the right model for the wrong project.

| | Fixed scope | Hourly billing (or retainer) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Price and timeline set for a list of deliverables | You pay for the hours the team puts in |
| Cost predictability | High, if the scope is well written | Low; depends on pace and changes |
| Flexibility to change | Changes become change orders | Change anytime |
| Who carries the risk of delays | The vendor | You |
| What it requires from you | Time up front to define the scope well | Constant tracking of hours and priorities |
| Best for | Projects with a clear goal: website, portal, system with a known workflow, integration | Products in continuous evolution, maintenance and improvements |

A common and healthy combination: the first version on fixed scope, with the essentials well defined, and later evolution on a monthly retainer, with priorities reviewed every cycle.

Be wary of "fixed scope" with an open scope. If the document does not list the deliverables, the fixed price protects no one.

## Contract clauses that protect you

The contract is where the relationship gets settled when things go wrong. Read these points carefully and, for larger contracts, have a lawyer review them.

**Code and intellectual property ownership.** The contract should state that the source code, the database, the screen designs and the documentation produced in the project belong to your company after payment. Watch out for libraries and components the vendor already had: it is normal for them to keep ownership of those, but they should grant you a permanent license to use them at no extra cost.

**Repository access.** Promising to hand over the code "at the end" is not enough. Ask for access to the repository (GitHub, GitLab or similar) from the start, preferably in an account owned by your company. That way you follow real progress and do not depend on anyone's goodwill to receive what you already paid for.

**Documentation.** Set the minimum: how to install and run the system, how to deploy it, which external services it uses and where the credentials live. Without this, any other vendor will charge weeks just to understand what exists.

**Warranty period.** A period after delivery in which defects (what does not work as specified) are fixed at no cost. Make it clear that the warranty covers bugs, and that new features are a separate matter.

**Exit and transition.** The clause nobody reads and everybody needs. It answers: if the contract ends, how quickly does the vendor hand over code, data, hosting access and domains? Do they help with the handoff to another team? Is there a penalty? Systems hosted in the vendor's account with no exit clause are the most common way a company ends up locked in.

Other points worth putting in writing: confidentiality, handling of personal data under the applicable privacy law, who pays for hosting and licenses, and the process for requesting and approving scope changes.

## How to evaluate a software company's portfolio and references

A nice portfolio shows the company can make nice screens. You need to know whether it delivers systems that work.

- **Ask for projects similar to yours.** A good website vendor is not necessarily good at ERP integration, and vice versa.
- **Open whatever is live.** Test public websites and systems on your phone. Check that they load fast and that the forms work.
- **Ask what went wrong.** Every project has problems. The answer shows how the company deals with them.
- **Talk to real customers.** Ask for two or three contacts and ask them: was the timeline met? Did the scope change? What was it like to ask for fixes? Did you receive the code? Would you hire them again?
- **Ask who is still a customer.** Projects still maintained by the same vendor years later say a lot.
- **Be wary of logos without context.** A list of big brands with no explanation of what was done may be a small or subcontracted job.

## Questions to ask in the first meeting

Bring this list. The answers are worth more than the sales deck.

- What did you understand about our problem? What would you do differently from what we asked?
- What would you cut from the first version to deliver sooner?
- Who will work on the project, and is that person in this meeting?
- How does communication work: how often do we see progress?
- Will we have a staging environment to test before going to production?
- How do you test the code?
- Will the code live in a repository we own from day one?
- Where will the system be hosted, and whose name is the account in?
- What happens if we want to switch vendors two years from now?
- How are change requests handled mid-project?
- What does the warranty cover, and for how long?
- Can we talk to two customers with similar projects?

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to hire a software development company?

It depends on the number of screens and business rules, the integrations with other systems, the team involved and the timeline. That is why proposals for the "same" system vary so much: they usually describe different things. Before comparing prices, put the scopes side by side and see what each one includes and excludes.

### Is it better to hire a freelancer or a software company?

A freelancer can work well for small, well-defined projects. The risk is continuity: if that person becomes unavailable, nobody else knows the system. A company tends to offer more stability, a testing process and coverage during vacations and emergencies. Either way, the clauses on code, repository and documentation apply just the same.

### Do I need to know how to code to oversee the project?

No. You need to follow the deliverables, test in the staging environment and confirm the system does what the business needs. To assess technical quality, an [independent consultant](/servicos/consultoria-tech) can review the code and architecture at key points in the project.

### What should I do if my current vendor will not hand over the source code?

Reread the contract to see what was agreed about ownership and exit. If the code is yours under the contract, make the request in writing with a deadline. If the contract says nothing, negotiate the handover as part of ending the relationship. And on your next project, require repository access from day one.

### Custom system or off-the-shelf software?

If off-the-shelf software fits your process with small adjustments, it is usually faster and cheaper. A [custom system](/servicos/sistemas-web) makes sense when the process is what sets your company apart or when you need to connect several systems that do not talk to each other. Often the answer is a mix: an off-the-shelf ERP with custom [integrations](/blog/como-integrar-com-erp).
