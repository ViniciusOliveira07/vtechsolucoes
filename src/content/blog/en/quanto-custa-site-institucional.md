You ask three agencies for a website quote and get three numbers that have nothing to do with each other. One looks too cheap, another looks too expensive and the third doesn't explain what's included. There's no way to tell what a fair price is.

The honest answer to "how much does a business website cost" is: it depends on what's inside the proposal. This article shows what drives the cost of a company website, the pricing models, the costs that show up later and a list of questions to compare proposals on equal terms.

## Why website quotes vary so much

Two quotes with the same name, "business website," can describe completely different jobs. One might be a ready-made template with your logo and five pages of copy you write yourself. Another might include keyword research, copy written for Google, custom design, CRM integration and lead tracking.

The price changes because the work changes. The problem is that most proposals describe what they deliver vaguely ("responsive website with SEO") and say nothing about what's left out. So before you look at the number, look at the scope.

## What drives the cost of a business website

There are seven main factors. The more of them a proposal covers, the higher the investment, and the higher the chance the website brings in customers.

**Number and type of pages.** It's not just the count. A complete service page, with the problem, the process, FAQs and a call to request a quote, takes far more work than a three-paragraph "About" page. A website with one page per service and location pages costs more than a five-page website, but it also shows up in many more searches.

**Content production.** This is the most underestimated factor. Someone has to write the copy, and it has to answer buyers' questions and the searches people run on Google. If the content is up to you, the project is cheaper and usually runs late, because the copy becomes the bottleneck. Professional photos, videos and illustrations belong here too.

**Design.** A ready-made template is faster and cheaper. A custom design, built for your brand and to guide visitors toward contacting you, takes more hours of design and review. The common middle ground is to start from a well-built component system and adapt it to your company's identity.

**Technical SEO and speed.** Heading structure, clean URLs, a sitemap, structured data, Search Console set up and a website that's fast on mobile. Google's "good" thresholds for Core Web Vitals are public: LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP within 200 milliseconds and CLS below 0.1. Hitting them depends on how the website is built, not on a plugin installed at the end. If you want to understand each item, the guide on [how to build a professional website that ranks on Google](/blog/como-criar-site-profissional-que-aparece-no-google) covers it step by step.

**Integrations.** A form that goes straight into your CRM, a WhatsApp alert for the sales team, online booking, a catalog pulled from your ERP. Each integration has its own cost, which depends on the quality of the other system's API. A form that only sends an email is simple. A form that creates an opportunity in the CRM and fires an alert takes more. When connecting to other systems is central, it's worth treating it as an [integration](/servicos/integracoes) project.

**Hosting and infrastructure.** Shared hosting, a dedicated server or an edge platform with a CDN. The choice affects speed, security and the monthly cost. Static or server-rendered websites are usually cheap to host and fast to load.

**Maintenance and updates.** After launch, someone has to update dependencies, fix bugs, publish new content and adjust pages. This can be included in a monthly fee, billed by the hour or simply not exist, and that last case is the one that causes the most trouble.

## Pricing models: fixed-price project or monthly fee

There are two main formats, each with pros and cons:

| | Fixed-price project | Monthly fee |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | One price for a defined scope | Monthly payment covering the website and ongoing services |
| Upfront investment | Higher | Lower or none |
| Cost over the years | Predictable; maintenance billed separately | Sum of the monthly fees for as long as the contract runs |
| Ownership | The code is usually handed over to the client | The website often stays on the provider's platform |
| If you cancel | The website is still yours | You may lose the website and have to rebuild it |
| Best for | Those who want an asset of their own and a clear scope | Those who need to spread the investment and want ongoing support |

Neither model is wrong. The point is knowing what happens when the contract ends. Always ask: if I stop paying, do the website, the domain and the copy still belong to me?

## Hidden costs that show up later

Many cheap proposals become expensive because they leave out items you'll pay for anyway. Check whether the quote mentions each one:

- **Domain.** It renews every year. It must be registered under your company's registration number, not the provider's name.
- **Hosting.** Monthly or yearly. Ask whether it's included, for how long and what it costs afterward.
- **Business email.** Accounts on your company domain are billed separately in most cases.
- **Content.** If copy and photos aren't in scope, someone will have to produce them, and that someone is usually you.
- **Maintenance.** Security updates, fixes and small changes. Without it, the website ages and can become vulnerable.
- **Migration.** If you already have a website, switching without redirecting the old URLs tanks your Google rankings. URL mapping and 301 redirects need to be planned.
- **Licenses.** Themes, paid plugins and fonts may renew every year.
- **Out-of-scope changes.** Find out how many rounds of revisions are included and how anything beyond that is billed.

## Cheap proposal or complete proposal: what changes

The price difference is almost always explained by this table:

| Item | Cheap proposal (typical) | Complete proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Pages | Few, with services lumped into one | One page per main service |
| Copy | Provided by the client | Written for the customer and for search |
| Design | Ready-made template with your logo | Adapted to the brand and focused on conversion |
| SEO | "Optimized website," no details | Titles, sitemap, structured data and Search Console |
| Speed | Not measured | Core Web Vitals checked on mobile |
| Form | Sends an email | Integrated with the CRM or the sales team's WhatsApp |
| Tracking | None | Google Analytics 4 tracking contacts |
| Migration | Not planned | URL mapping and 301 redirects |
| Ownership | Website locked into the provider's platform | Code, domain and access handed over to the client |
| Timeline and scope | Open-ended | Fixed scope and a set delivery date |

Not every company needs the entire right-hand column. A freelancer who only wants an online business card can do fine with something simple. But if the website exists to generate quote requests, every item missing from the left-hand column becomes a reason for it not to work.

## How to compare website proposals: questions to ask

Take this list to every conversation with a provider. The answers show what's actually in the price:

1. **Which pages are included?** Ask for the list, page by page.
2. **Who writes the copy?** And who provides the photos?
3. **What does "SEO" mean in this proposal?** Ask for the items: titles, sitemap, structured data, Search Console.
4. **How do you make sure the website is fast on mobile?** The answer should mention measurement, not a promise.
5. **Which integrations are included?** CRM, WhatsApp, booking, ERP.
6. **How many rounds of revisions are included?** And how is a change billed after that?
7. **What's the timeline and what happens if it slips?** A timeline without a date and without a fixed scope tends to stretch.
8. **Is the domain registered under my company's registration number?**
9. **Do I get the code and the logins?** Hosting, domain, Analytics, Search Console.
10. **What happens if I cancel or want to switch providers?**
11. **What are the recurring costs, item by item?** Hosting, domain, licenses, maintenance.
12. **If I already have a website, how is the migration handled?** Ask about redirects.

With the answers in hand, build a spreadsheet with one row per item and one column per provider. Often the most expensive proposal stops being the most expensive once you add up what the other one doesn't include.

## Red flags in a website quote

- **A promise of Google's first page.** Nobody controls rankings. A technically sound website can be guaranteed, a position can't.
- **A one-line scope.** "Responsive business website" doesn't say what you'll get.
- **A domain registered in the provider's name.** When it's time to switch, you're held hostage.
- **No mention of content.** It means content is your problem.
- **No set timeline.** Projects without a date tend to never end.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to maintain a business website each month?

The monthly cost adds up hosting, domain renewal (spread over the year), business email, licenses and maintenance. Lightweight websites without paid plugins usually have low recurring costs. Ask the provider for the list of recurring costs before you sign.

### Is a website builder cheaper?

At first, yes. Over the years, the platform's monthly fee, plugins and customization limits can add up. Compare the total cost over two or three years, not just the entry price.

### Is it better to pay for the website upfront or monthly?

It depends on your cash flow and what you want to own at the end. A fixed-price project usually leaves the website as your asset. A monthly fee spreads the investment, but check whether the website remains yours if the contract ends.

### How long does it take to build a business website?

A business website with a well-defined scope can be delivered in a few weeks. What causes the most delays is content production and approval rounds, so it's worth deciding who writes the copy right at the start.

### Why can one business website cost more than another with the same number of pages?

Because the page count is just one factor. Content produced by the provider, custom design, technical SEO, integrations and tracking change the work, even with the same number of pages.
