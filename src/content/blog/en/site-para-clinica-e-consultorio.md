A patient gets a toothache on Sunday night, or finally decides to see a therapist, or gets a physical therapy referral from an orthopedist. Very often, the next step is the same: searching Google. "Dentist in Coral Gables," "online therapist," "physical therapy near me." Whoever shows up with clear information and a simple way to book gets the call.

A clinic website isn't a digital brochure. It's where patients confirm that the professional is legitimate, understand how care works and book an appointment. This guide covers what the website of a medical, dental, psychology, physical therapy or esthetics practice needs, how to respect professional advertising rules, how to protect patient data and how to show up in local searches.

## Why a clinic needs a website, not just a social media profile

Social media helps show your day-to-day and educate people who already follow you. But someone looking for a healthcare provider for the first time almost always starts on Google, and social media content rarely shows up in those searches.

Healthcare is also a decision based on trust. Patients want to know who will see them, what their training is, where the office is, whether their insurance is accepted and how to book. An organized website answers all of that in one place, on an address you own that doesn't depend on any platform's algorithm. ## What a clinic website needs

### A homepage that answers the basic questions

On the first screen, patients need to understand: what the specialty is, where you are, whether you offer in-person care, telehealth or both, and how to book. Avoid generic lines like "caring for you with excellence." Go for something concrete: "Orthopedic and sports physical therapy in Coral Gables."

### Pages per specialty and per treatment

This is what makes the biggest difference in search. A multi-specialty clinic should have one page for each specialty. A single-specialty practice can have pages for its main treatments or the conditions it treats.

Each page should explain:

- What the specialty or treatment is and when to seek it.
- What the first visit is like and what to bring.
- Which of the clinic's professionals work in that area.
- Frequently asked questions, in plain language.
- How to book.

The copy should be informative and responsible. The goal is to help patients understand, not to promise a cure or a result.

### Pages per professional

Patients choose people, not just brands. Create a page for each professional with:

- Full name and a real photo.
- Professional license number and, where applicable, board certification in the specialty.
- Education, specializations and areas of practice.
- Days and locations where they see patients.
- A booking button that goes straight to that professional.

These pages also help people who search for the professional's name after getting a referral.

### Insurance, fees and types of care

One of the first questions patients ask is "do you take my insurance?" Make the list of accepted plans easy to find and keep it up to date. If you only see self-pay patients, say so clearly and explain whether you provide receipts for reimbursement. Also say whether you offer telehealth and how it works.

### Address, access and facilities

Include the full address, an embedded map, phone number, hours and a button that opens directions on the phone. Mention parking, accessibility and how to get there by public transit. Real photos of the reception area and treatment rooms help patients feel more comfortable before the first visit.

## Booking: fewer steps between search and appointment

The website doesn't need its own scheduling system. The best route is usually to connect the site to what the clinic already uses:

- **Online scheduling or practice management software.** If it offers a booking link, the site should go straight to the right specialty or professional.
- **WhatsApp or text with a pre-filled message.** The button opens the conversation already saying which page the patient came from, for example "Hi, I'd like to book a physical therapy evaluation." When message volume is high, you can automate triage and repetitive questions, as we show in our guide to an [AI agent on WhatsApp](/blog/agente-de-ia-no-whatsapp).
- **A tap-to-call phone number** on mobile, for people who prefer to call.

A few rules apply to any route: a visible booking button on every page, the same link on the website, social media and Business Profile, and a periodic test done as a patient, from Google to a confirmed appointment.

## Trust: visible license numbers and clear information

In healthcare, trust comes before design. What matters most:

- **License numbers visible** on each professional's page and in the footer, along with the name of the clinical director, when the clinic has one.
- **Verifiable credentials**, with no vague or inflated titles.
- **Content written or reviewed by the professional**, with the reviewer's name.
- **Real reviews** from your Google Business Profile, unedited and with no made-up testimonials.
- **A clear privacy policy** explaining what happens to the data submitted through the site.

## Professional advertising rules: what to watch on your website

Every healthcare profession is regulated by a professional board or council, and each one has its own advertising rules. Your website counts as advertising, so those rules apply to it, including copy, photos and calls to action. Broadly, the points that come up across boards are:

- **Identifying the professional**, with name and license number in ads and on the website.
- **No promises or guarantees of results.** Phrases like "guaranteed results" or "permanent cure" are a problem.
- **Care with patient images and before-and-after photos.** Some professions ban them, others allow them only for educational purposes under specific criteria. Sensational images used just to attract patients are usually prohibited.
- **No sensationalism or excessive self-promotion**, such as calling yourself "the best" or "the only one."
- **Specialties only when officially recognized.** Advertising a specialty you aren't registered or certified in is usually a violation.

In Brazil, where Vtech is based, some examples of rules in force are:

- **Medicine:** the Federal Council of Medicine regulates medical advertising in [CFM Resolution No. 2,336/2023](https://sistemas.cfm.org.br/normas/arquivos/resolucoes/BR/2023/2336_2023.pdf), which covers, among other points, the use of patient images and prohibits promising results.
- **Dentistry:** the Federal Council of Dentistry addresses advertising in its Code of Dental Ethics, approved by Resolution CFO-118/2012 and amended by later resolutions.
- **Psychology:** the Federal Council of Psychology sets its advertising rules in the [Code of Professional Ethics for Psychologists](https://site.cfp.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/codigo-de-etica-psicologia.pdf).
- **Physical therapy and esthetics:** the physical therapy council and the other boards that regulate esthetics professionals have their own codes and rules.

In other countries, the equivalent rules come from state or national licensing boards and professional associations. Rules change over time and each has details that don't fit here. Before publishing your website, read your board's current rules and, when in doubt, contact the board directly. This article is general guidance and doesn't replace that check.

## Privacy: handle the data patients send through your website with care

Health data gets special protection in many privacy laws. In Brazil, the [General Data Protection Law (Law No. 13,709/2018)](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2018/lei/l13709compilado.htm) classifies health data as sensitive personal data, with stricter processing rules. Wherever you are, check the privacy and health-data laws that apply to your practice. In practice, this directly affects your website forms:

- **Ask only for what you need.** To book, a name, phone number and the desired specialty are usually enough. Avoid open fields like "describe your symptoms" on public forms.
- **State the purpose** next to the form: what the data will be used for and by whom.
- **Have an accessible privacy policy**, with contact details for whoever is responsible for the data.
- **Use a secure connection (HTTPS)** across the whole site.
- **Watch where the data goes.** A form that lands in an inbox shared by the whole team or in an open spreadsheet is a risk. Send it to the clinic's system instead, with restricted access.
- **Cookies and analytics tools** must be disclosed to visitors. Avoid sending ad platforms any information that reveals the health condition of someone who visited a page.

If the clinic already has a data protection officer or legal counsel, involve them in reviewing the website before it goes live.

## Local SEO for clinics and private practices

People looking for a dentist, therapist or physical therapist almost always want someone near home or work. That's why local SEO matters so much:

- **A complete Google Business Profile**, with the most specific category (dentist, psychologist, physical therapist), hours, real photos, a booking link and replies to reviews.
- **Identical name, address and phone number** on the website, the profile and social media.
- **Neighborhood and city in the copy and page titles**, naturally: "Orthodontics in Coral Gables" tells Google and the patient exactly what they'll find.
- **One page per location**, if the clinic has more than one address, with real information about that location.
- **Structured data** of type `MedicalClinic`, `Dentist` or `Physician` from Schema.org, with name, address, phone and hours.
- **A fast website on mobile**, because many people search on their phones, and that's where patients give up if the page is slow.

## Website for a dentist, therapist and other specialties: what changes

- **Dentist website:** pages per treatment (implants, orthodontics, whitening, pediatric dentistry), extra care with case images, and emergency care highlighted if you offer it.
- **Psychologist or therapist website:** welcoming language, a clear explanation of your approach, telehealth and confidentiality. Even leaner forms, without asking people to describe how they're feeling.
- **Medical practice website:** pages per condition treated and per test or procedure, visible specialty credentials and clear guidance on preparing for appointments and tests.
- **Physical therapy:** pages per area (orthopedic, neurological, pelvic, sports), an explanation of how treatment works and whether you offer home visits.
- **Esthetics:** explanatory copy for each procedure, no promised results, and clear identification of the licensed professional who performs each one.

## Common mistakes on clinic websites

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| No license number shown | May go against board rules and reduces trust |
| Promised results or sensational before-and-after photos | Prohibited or restricted by the rules of several boards |
| All specialties on a single page | The site doesn't show up in searches by specialty |
| Forms asking for symptoms and medical history | Collects sensitive data unnecessarily |
| Outdated insurance list | Causes frustration and lost calls |
| Booking button hidden on mobile | Patients give up before booking |

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a small private practice need a website?

Yes, if you want new patients to find you on Google. A lean website with a homepage, pages for your main treatments, a page for the professional, insurance, contact and booking is enough.

### Can I show before-and-after photos on my clinic's website?

It depends on your profession and your board's rules. Some ban them, others allow them under specific conditions and for educational purposes. Read your board's current rules and, when in doubt, contact the board before publishing.

### Do I need consent on the booking form?

The form should clearly state what the data will be used for and link to the privacy policy. The right way to handle each piece of data depends on the legal basis your clinic relies on, so review the form with whoever handles privacy compliance at your practice.

### Can a clinic website show consultation fees?

Some professions allow it, others restrict it. The rule varies from board to board and has changed over time, so check your board's current rules before publishing prices.

### How long does it take for a clinic website to show up on Google?

Once it's published and submitted to Google Search Console, the site usually shows up in searches for the clinic's name fairly quickly. Ranking well for specialty searches in your area depends on local competition and consistent work on the website and Business Profile.
